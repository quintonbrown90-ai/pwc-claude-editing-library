## The rule
Retention is not density — it is pleasure riding a wave. The viewer's
brain scans for change (pattern interrupt), for feeling (emotional
progression), and for meaning (a device that arrives ON the thing it
illustrates). An edit that changes the screen every 2 seconds with no
emotional direction is noise; an edit with feeling but no change is a
lecture. Both fail. The edit must do three things at once: move, feel,
mean.

## The mechanics (each with its psychological job)
1. **Pattern interrupt (the 0.3s grab).** Stanford neuroscience, via
   tukey.ai: when the brain detects something that violates expectation,
   it triggers a 0.3-second attention grab and reallocates attention to
   the anomaly. A cut, a zoom kick, a text pop, a sound — each is a
   micro-violation of the expected frame. Rule: a visual event every
   ≤2s (Q's standing rule, independently confirmed by Bradford's "every
   2–3 seconds"). The interrupt must be MOTIVATED — a zoom on a payoff
   word, a pop-up on the exact spoken thing — never a zoom into nothing
   (Shane Hummus failure #2).
2. **Emotional progression (the 2× multiplier).** Videos with a clear
   emotional progression retain 2× more than purely informational ones.
   Map the feeling per beat before mapping the devices: frustration →
   relief → proof (Q's synchrony rule from the four-levers part). A
   device that lands on the wrong feeling is worse than no device — a
   celebration sting on a frustration beat reads as mockery.
3. **Promise before how (the payoff-first structure).** Bradford's
   breakdown: "You see the finished doc at 0:07, so you stay through
   the setup to get it." Q's proof-in-first-2s rule is the same mechanic
   in reel form. The viewer must SEE the destination before they will
   walk the road. Never teach the steps before showing the result.
4. **Value builds in layers.** Bradford's "Why it worked" #3: "The
   value builds in layers: hook, then the result, then the steps, then
   [bonus]." Each layer is a small payoff that earns the next 10
   seconds. A video that delivers everything at once has nothing left
   to watch for; a video that delivers nothing until the end loses
   everyone before the end.
5. **One memorable detail per beat.** Jack Roberts: "A cassette player
   with spinning wheels. A delete button you have to hold. A checkout
   that prints a receipt. Small details people remember." The detail is
   what the viewer describes to a friend later. One per beat, physical,
   specific — a gold circle drawn around the proof figure, a receipt
   printing, a stamp slamming. Generic motion is forgotten by the next
   scroll.
6. **Sound as feeling, not decoration.** The pleasure test covers sound:
   a tick/pop on every caption word, a sound on every pop-up and
   diagram, a low cleared bed under the voice. Sound tells the viewer
   how to FEEL about what just appeared (Bradford's "Reveal + modeled
   reaction": "It's wild" tells you how to feel about it). Silence is
   also a device — but only as a dramatic pause (max 2/video, ≤0.30s),
   never as dead air.
7. **End on the payoff so it feels finished.** Eddie AI's clipper rule:
   a clip must "end on the payoff so the clip actually feels finished
   instead of just cutting it through." A video that trails off after
   the payoff feels broken; a video that ends ON the payoff feels
   complete — and a complete feeling is what makes the viewer watch the
   next one. Last-voice → end-card ≤1s (standing ending gate).

## Executable steps
1. Before the edit, write the emotional beat map: one feeling word per
   section (frustration / curiosity / relief / proof / celebration).
   No feeling word = no section.
2. Check the change cadence on the exported file: a visual event every
   ≤2s, a device landing ON its spoken trigger word, a zoom only on a
   face/gesture/payoff beat. Any stretch >2.5s with no event gets a
   device or a cut.
3. Check promise-before-how: is the result/proof visible before the
   steps begin? If the steps come first, reorder.
4. Check the layer count: hook → result → steps → bonus → CTA is five
   layers. Fewer than three layers = the video is one flat note.
5. Check the memorable detail: can you name one physical detail per
   beat that a viewer would describe to a friend? If not, add one.
6. Check the ending: last spoken word → end card ≤1s, and the end card
   lands ON the payoff, not after a tail of bed-only footage.
