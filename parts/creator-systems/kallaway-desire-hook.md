## The rule
Outcome + character. The hook names the outcome the viewer secretly wants
(not the information, the OUTCOME), then ties it to a relatable character
(you, the viewer, or a third party). The info hook ("three best algorithm
hacks") gets views from low-quality viewers who never buy; the desire hook
("200 to 20,000 followers in six weeks") gets buyers. The two-part check is
the gate: outcome named? character tied?

## Executable steps
1. The Desire Hook template (2 lines): Line 1 — "Here's how [character]
   went from [base state] to [desired outcome] in [timeframe]." Line 2 —
   the non-obvious solution (the thing they don't know yet).
2. The info-hook test: write the info version first ("Here are the 3 best
   ___"), then the desire version. If the desire version does not name a
   concrete outcome + a character, it is still an info hook — rewrite it.
3. The buyer test: "If that solution is not obvious, a lot of people will
   want to buy it." — if the solution IS obvious, the hook attracts viewers,
   not buyers. Q's hooks must attract waitlisters (buyers), not just viewers.
4. The character rule: the character is Q, the viewer ("you"), or a real
   third party (a creator with a link — never an invented "Tony" without a
   source; his "Tony went from 220,000 followers" is an example, not a
   verified case — Q's characters must trace to the proof library).

## Worked example (PWC)
Info hook: "Here are the 3 best ways to price your UGC." Desire hook:
"Here's how you go from posting for free to your first $1,000 brand deal in
90 days." Character: you (the viewer). Solution (non-obvious): the pitch
system inside Paid With Content. Keyword WAITLIST.

## Human-made check
The outcome must be one Q's system actually delivers (90 days, deal-ready,
$97/mo) — never a promised number Q cannot stand behind. The character must
be real.

## PWC skin
Second-person framing ("you"), collective framing for earnings ("creators
like my wife and I, and other creators…"), never a direct solo claim.
